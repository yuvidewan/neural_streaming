# Research notes and next plans (2026-11-14)

Where we are: **+69.1% vs x264** with real bytes (learned P-frame coder, Vimeo chunks 1-10,
I-frame every 30). That is about 1.7x x264's bits at the same quality. P-frames are roughly 94% of
the stream at GOP 30 (estimated from the measured P/I ratio of ~0.58, not counted directly). Our model: 8.7M weights (encoder 3.8M, synthesis 3.6M, prior 1.2M,
refine 0.2M, context 0.04M).

Everything below marked **[paper]** is a claim from the cited source, read from search
summaries, not from a full read of the paper. Everything marked **[ours]** is my judgement.
No number here is measured on our codec.

## Part 0. The most important finding: it comes from our own numbers, not from a paper

I split our GOP-30 result by test video (`gate3_stage3_gop30.json` against the stored x264
runs). **These numbers are measured.**

| Video | Us vs x264 | Our quality range | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| drone | **-20.6%** (we win) | 35.1 → 37.5 dB | |
| surf | **-5.3%** (we win) | 34.2 → 35.7 dB | |
| gold-fish | n/a | 34.0 → 36.4 dB | We are above x264's whole measured range (it tops out at 33.5 dB). |
| cat-girl | +25.3% | 31.4 → 33.0 dB | |
| bmx-bumps | +71.6% | 33.1 → 35.0 dB | |
| drift-chicane | +101.2% | 34.8 → 36.9 dB | |
| car-turn | +139.6% | 31.9 → 33.3 dB | |
| **cows** | **+292.1%** | **27.8 → 28.7 dB** | Stuck. |
| **schoolgirls** | **+292.3%** | **28.0 → 28.5 dB** | Stuck. |

**We already beat x264 on 2 of 9 videos, and the average is wrecked by two videos where
our quality will not rise.**

- On *cows*, going from 0.32 to 0.68 bpp (2.1x the bits) buys only +0.86 dB. On
  *schoolgirls*, 2.2x the bits buys +0.45 dB.
- At about the same bits, x264 is far ahead there: cows at 0.305 bpp is 31.5 dB for x264,
  and 27.8 dB for us at 0.321 bpp. **That is 3.7 dB behind.**
- x264 keeps climbing on those videos (to 32.6 and 33.3 dB). We hit a ceiling near
  28.5 dB whatever we spend.

**What this means [ours]:**
- Our problem is not mainly "P-frames cost too many bits". It is a **quality ceiling on
  finely detailed content** (grass, patterned clothing, at 256x256). More bits do not turn
  into more quality there.
- It fits earlier measurements: the old quantizers capped near 32.6 dB on average, and the
  latent is 64 channels at 1/16 resolution with whole-number rounding.
- The entropy-model ideas (Part 8) make the *same* quality cheaper. They do not lift a
  ceiling. So they are not the first thing to do.
- **I do not yet know which part causes the ceiling.** Candidates: the encoder, the
  decoder's capacity, the rounding step, or the I-frames that seed each chain.

### Plan 0. Find the ceiling's cause (half a day, no training) — do this first

On *cows* and *schoolgirls* only, with the stage 3 coder:
1. **Latent refinement with the rate term off.** Optimize each frame's latent directly for
   quality. If PSNR rises well past 28.5 dB, the **encoder** is the limit (fix: encoder
   tuning or refinement, cheap).
2. **No rounding.** Decode from the unrounded latent. If PSNR jumps, the **rounding step**
   is the limit (fix: learned quantization step, idea 31).
3. **Decoder tuning on that one video.** If only this lifts it, the **decoder's capacity**
   is the limit (fix: 1/8-resolution latent or more channels, i.e. an architecture change
   and a Vimeo retrain).
4. **I-frame check.** Report I-frame PSNR against P-frame PSNR on these videos. If the
   I-frames sit at ~28 dB, the **intra model** is the ceiling and the P-coder inherits it.

Each outcome points to a different fix, so this half day decides the order of everything
else in this document.

**Rough size of the prize [ours]:** if those two videos reached x264's quality at equal
bits (+3 to +4 dB), the 9-video mean PSNR would rise about 0.7-0.8 dB at the same bitrate.
On x264's curve, 1 dB is worth roughly 20-25% of the bits here, so that alone is on the
order of **-15% to -20%**, from two videos.

### Plan 0, first result (measured 13:55): the ceiling is in the transform itself

I ran the I-frame model on every 10th frame of four test videos, once with normal rounding
and once with **no rounding at all** (as if bits were free).

| Video | λ | Rounded | No rounding |
|---|---|---|---|
| cows | 1e-3 | 26.72 dB | 27.41 dB |
| cows | 1e-4 | 27.51 dB | **27.61 dB** |
| schoolgirls | 1e-3 | 27.45 dB | 28.31 dB |
| schoolgirls | 1e-4 | 28.50 dB | **28.62 dB** |
| drone | 1e-4 | 35.96 dB | 36.37 dB |
| surf | 1e-4 | 34.78 dB | 35.05 dB |

- **Even with unlimited bits, the model cannot show *cows* better than 27.6 dB or
  *schoolgirls* better than 28.6 dB.** Rounding costs only 0.1 dB at the best setting, so
  the rounding step is not the cause (test 2 of Plan 0: answered, no).
- The limit is the **encode→decode transform**: 64 numbers describe each 16x16 patch of
  768 colour values, a 12-to-1 squeeze. Fine texture needs more numbers than that, and
  x264 can spend bits on every detail coefficient.
- The P-frame coder was started from this same transform and has the same shape. That is
  why its quality sits just above these values (28.5-28.7 dB) however many bits it spends.
- This was measured on the I-frame model only. The P-coder's own no-rounding number and
  the encoder-vs-decoder split (tests 1 and 3) are still to do.

### Plan 0, third result (measured 13:59): it is the decoder side, not the encoder

For the I-frame model at λ=1e-4, I searched directly for the best possible latent for 4
frames per video (400 optimizer steps, rate ignored), instead of using the encoder's.

| Video | Encoder's latent | Best latent found | Gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| cows | 27.56 dB | 28.19 dB | +0.63 dB |
| schoolgirls | 28.58 dB | 29.42 dB | +0.84 dB |
| drone | 34.96 dB | 36.28 dB | +1.32 dB |

- **No latent makes the decoder draw *cows* better than about 28.2 dB.** The limit is what
  a 64-channel, 1/16-resolution latent plus this decoder can express. The encoder is
  already close to the best it could do.
- This answers tests 1 and 3 of Plan 0: a better encoder (or latent refinement) is worth
  roughly +0.6 to +1.3 dB at free bits and less at real bitrates. **The decoder/latent
  capacity is the ceiling.** Plan A is the fix.
- It also sizes Plan 3 (latent refinement): useful, but small on the videos that hurt most.
- Caveat: 400 steps may not be the true optimum, and this is 4 frames per video.

### Plan 0, second result (measured 13:56): the P-coder mostly copies its reference

P-frame coder at λ=1e-4 on the first 11 P-frames of three videos, each frame coded once from
its previous frame (single step, not chained):

| Video | Normal (rounded, I-frame-quality reference) | No rounding | No rounding **and a perfect previous frame** |
|---|---|---|---|
| cows | 27.90 dB | 27.97 dB | **30.90 dB** |
| schoolgirls | 29.20 dB | 29.28 dB | **34.22 dB** |
| drone | 32.89 dB | 33.07 dB | **35.77 dB** |

- **Rounding is not the limit for the P-coder either** (+0.1 to +0.2 dB with free bits).
- **Reference quality is nearly everything.** Hand the coder the *original* previous frame
  and it gains 3-5 dB. Hand it our I-frame-quality frame and it adds only ~0.4-0.8 dB on
  top of that frame.
- **The coder cannot add much new detail by itself.** Even from a perfect previous frame
  with free bits, *cows* comes out at 30.9 dB: the coder loses detail it was handed. Chained
  over a GOP, quality settles at the coder's own steady level (~28.7 dB on cows in the real
  run).
- Scope: 3 videos, 11 frames each, one rate point. It is a diagnosis, not a benchmark.

**What follows [ours]:**
1. **Plan A (more latent capacity) is confirmed as the main fix**, for both the I-frame model
   and the P-coder.
2. **Reference quality is a second, separate lever.** Anything that hands the P-coder a
   better previous frame pays off across the whole chain:
   - **Higher-quality I-frames.** They are only ~6% of the bits at GOP 30, so spending 2x
     on them is cheap. The gain fades along the chain, so it pairs with idea 5:
   - **Periodic high-quality frames** (hierarchical quality): refresh the reference every
     few frames.
   - **The x264 hybrid (Plan 5) looks stronger than I rated it.** On *cows*, x264 at 0.3 bpp
     is 31.5 dB, which is a far better reference than our own 27.5 dB I-frames.
3. **Entropy-model work stays valuable but second**: it makes a given quality cheaper, and
   this shows our quality is the problem on the worst videos.

**Literature check on the capacity explanation** **[paper]** [63]: image-codec papers
routinely use more latent channels for high-quality models (192 for low rates, 320 for
high), and several attribute high-rate saturation to "insufficient transform capacity".
One paper found a small effect from channel count in a different setting, so the size of
the gain for us has to be measured (Plan A gate 1).

### Plan 5's oracle, run early (measured 13:57): the x264 hybrid does not dodge the ceiling

x264 codes each video at a low quality. Today's P-coder (no fine-tuning) then codes every
frame using the x264 picture of the same frame as its reference. Total = x264 bits + ours.

| Video | x264 base | Hybrid (λ=1e-3) | x264 alone at about the hybrid's quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| cows | 0.162 bpp, 29.35 dB | 0.379 bpp, 29.75 dB | ~0.18 bpp |
| schoolgirls | 0.140 bpp, 30.04 dB | 0.371 bpp, 30.83 dB | ~0.16 bpp |
| drone | 0.178 bpp, 32.23 dB | 0.312 bpp, 35.36 dB | ~0.34 bpp |

- **It fails the bar I set for it** (total within 30% of x264 alone) on *cows* and
  *schoolgirls*: the neural layer spends 0.2+ bpp there and adds under 1 dB. That is the
  same capacity ceiling again. On *drone* it roughly ties x264.
- The coder was never trained on this kind of reference, so a fine-tuned layer would do
  better. But untrained it gives no sign of an easy win, and it inherits the same weak
  spot. **The hybrid drops down the list until the capacity fix (Plan A) is in.**
- Scope: 3 videos, 2 x264 settings, x264 in its default mode with an I-frame every 10.

### The published capacity rule matches our measurement

DCVC-RT states the rule directly **[paper]** [7]: count the latent values per frame against
the frame's own 3·H·W colour values.
- 512 channels at 1/16 resolution = 2·H·W values. They judged this **too few** and saw
  compression suffer.
- 256 channels at 1/8 resolution = 4·H·W values. They judged this enough and chose it.
- **Ours: 64 channels at 1/16 = 0.25·H·W values.** That is 8x below the setting they
  rejected as too small, and 16x below the one they chose.

So Plan A's arms should be sized by this rule, not by habit:

| Arm | Latent | Values per frame | vs today |
|---|---|---|---|
| today | 64 ch at 1/16 | 0.25·H·W | 1x |
| A1 | 192 ch at 1/16 | 0.75·H·W | 3x |
| A2 | 64 ch at 1/16 + 32 ch at 1/8 | 0.75·H·W | 3x |
| A3 | 128 ch at 1/8 | 2·H·W | 8x |

A3 is the DCVC-RT shape and the biggest change (the transform loses one downsampling
stage, so it cannot warm-start cleanly). A1 and A2 can start from today's weights. Run A1
and A2 in gate 1, and take A3 to the Vimeo retrain if they show the ceiling moving.

Unused latent values must cost almost nothing, or this repeats the earlier 192-channel
failure. The learned per-value scale prior does that for P-frames. **The I-frame path
still uses the old fixed entropy stack, so the I-frame model needs the learned prior
too before it is widened.**

**So the first trained change should be a wider latent, not a better entropy model.**

### Plan A. Lift the ceiling: more latent capacity (replaces "entropy model v2" as step 1)

- **Why it failed before and why it can work now [ours]:** the earlier 192-channel run cost
  3.2x the bits for +0.43 dB, because the old entropy coder charged the same for every
  channel whether it was used or not. The learned P-coder has a per-value scale prior, so an
  unused channel costs almost nothing. Published codecs use 192-320 channels at 1/16, or
  1/8 resolution (DCVC-RT), for this reason.
- **Build (two arms):**
  - **A1: 64 → 192 channels at 1/16.** Warm start: the new encoder outputs and the
    synthesis's new input weights start at zero, so step 0 equals today's coder.
  - **A2: add a 1/8-resolution detail latent** (32 channels) beside the 1/16 one, coded with
    its own prior. Zero-initialized in the same way.
- **Gate 1 (about a day, laptop):** fine-tune plain, A1 and A2 equally on DAVIS TRAIN
  (the fairness-check recipe). Score on VAL-B **and report the quality reached with no
  rounding on the most detailed VAL-B video.**
  **Pass: P-frame BD-rate ≤ -10% vs the plain copy, and the no-rounding ceiling up by
  ≥ 1.5 dB on that video.**
- **Gate 2:** Vimeo retrain (1.5 days), including a wider I-frame model. **Gate 3:** real
  bytes, **> 8 points better** than +69.1%.
- **Risk:** a short DAVIS fine-tune may not be enough to fill new channels. A near-miss on
  BD-rate with a clearly higher ceiling should be read as "needs the full training".

## Part 1. What the best learned video codecs do that we do not

The reference line is Microsoft's DCVC family: DCVC → TCM → HEM → DC → FM → RT.

| What they do | Source | Do we have it? |
|---|---|---|
| Pass **features** from frame to frame, not just the decoded picture | DCVC-TCM [1] | **No.** We pass only the picture. |
| **Multi-scale** temporal context (several resolutions) | DCVC-TCM [1] | No. One 32-channel context at full resolution. |
| Entropy model that uses the **previous frame's latent** as a prior | DCVC-HEM [2] | **No.** |
| Spatial context inside the frame (two-pass "dual prior", later quadtree) | HEM [2], DC [3] | No. |
| Train on **long chains** (cascaded loss; up to 32 frames) | DCVC-FM [4], ECVC [5] | Partly. Unroll 3. |
| **Hierarchical quality**: every few frames gets more bits | DCVC-DC [3], HLVC [6] | No. Every P-frame has the same λ. |
| Periodic **feature refresh** so one I-frame can last forever | DCVC-FM [4] | No (but GOP 30 held up). |
| One model for all rates (quantization step as an input) | HEM [2], RT [7] | No. Three models. |
| **No motion at all**: implicit temporal context | DCVC-RT [7] | No. We send block motion. |
| Latent at 1/8 resolution, single scale, for speed | DCVC-RT [7] | Ours is 1/16. |

Key numbers **[paper]**:
- DCVC-HEM saves 57.1% vs the original DCVC on UVG. Most of that came from feature
  propagation plus the better entropy model [2].
- DCVC-RT: 1080p at ~125 fps encode / ~113 fps decode, 21% fewer bits than VTM (H.266) [7].
- DCVC-RT's own ablation: dropping motion costs only 3.2% on large-motion video and gains
  0.4% on small-motion video, with 3.4x faster encoding [7].
- In DCVC-RT the hyper-latent `z` is over 10% of the bits [7].
- The main cause of drift on long chains is training on short ones [5]. Fixes: longer
  cascaded training [4]; training in groups to save memory (ECVC's PCFS) [5]; a reference
  quality adaptation module (19 training frames were enough) [8].
- Independent benchmark: ECM still beats DCVC-FM by 11% in a low-delay test [9]. Published
  wins depend on the test setup.

**What this means for us [ours]:**
1. Our own motion test agrees with DCVC-RT: explicit motion matters little. Free dense
   flow saved at most 19.9%, and they lose only 3% by removing motion entirely.
2. **The single biggest gap is feature propagation.** Our P-coder gets a 3-channel picture.
   Theirs gets a 48-64 channel feature map that never went through an 8-bit bottleneck.
3. The second gap is the entropy model (previous-latent prior and spatial context).
4. Our hyperprior test finished at 14:00 and **did not pass**: -0.4% against a bar of -8%.
   The side information collapsed to 8 bits per frame (`outputs/p3_hyperprior/README.md`).
   That fits Part 0: on our worst videos the limit is quality, not the cost of bits.

## Part 2. CNN architectures we could use

| Idea | What it is | Evidence | Fit for us [ours] |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Depthwise-separable / ConvNeXt blocks** | Big cheap kernels (7x7 depthwise) + 1x1 mixing | ConvNeXt-ChARM beats older codecs at lower decoder cost [10]; DCVC-RT uses depthwise blocks [7] | **Good.** More receptive field per weight. Replace our ResidualBlocks. |
| **Channel mixing over spatial ops** | 1x1 convs do most of the work | S2CFormer: channel aggregation is the key, not complex spatial ops [11] | Good. Cheap to try. |
| **Asymmetric encoder/decoder** | Heavy encoder, light decoder | AsymLLIC [12]; shallow-decoder work cuts decoder MACs up to 90% [13] | **Good for "real-time".** Encoder can grow for free at decode time. |
| **Re-parameterization (RepVGG)** | Train with 3 parallel branches, merge into one 3x3 conv for deployment | Exact merge; +0.37-2.7 dB in a neural-video-representation paper [14] | **Very good fit**: free quality at zero decode cost. GDN layers stay as they are. |
| **ELIC-style uneven channel groups** | Code the latent's channels in 5 uneven groups, each conditioned on earlier ones | ELIC [15]; channel-wise models save 6.7-11.4% over a context baseline, up to 18% at low rate [16] | **Good.** Parallel, no slow autoregression. |
| **Checkerboard / dual spatial prior** | Code half the positions, then the other half given the first | Within ~4% of slow autoregressive context [17] | Good. Two passes, cheap. |
| **Quadtree partition** | Checkerboard + channel groups combined | DCVC-DC [3] | Later. It is the above two combined. |
| Swin/Transformer blocks (TCM) | Attention mixed with convs | Best RD on images, slow | **No.** Too slow for real-time on a laptop GPU. |
| Mamba/state-space blocks | Long-range mixing | New, unproven for speed | No. |

**Recommendation [ours]:** depthwise blocks + re-parameterization for the transforms, and a
checkerboard + channel-group entropy model. No transformers.

## Part 3. Train a very large model, then prune it down?

**Short answer: partly yes, but pruning by itself is the weakest form of the idea.**

Evidence:
- **Against plain pruning:** "Rethinking the Value of Network Pruning" found that for
  structured pruning (removing whole channels, which is the kind that actually speeds things
  up), training the small network from scratch matches or beats train-big-then-prune. The
  value of pruning is mostly in *finding the shape* of the small network [18].
- **For it, in compression specifically:**
  - EVC "mask decay" (Microsoft, ICLR 2023): train a large codec, then slowly decay masks
    so the large model's weights turn into the small model's. It reports closing 50% / 30%
    of the gap for the medium / small model. The small model runs 1080p at 30 fps [19].
  - Structured pruning of learned image codecs with **no BD-rate loss** at smaller size [20].
  - Pruning the hyperprior alone removed 22.6% of weights and improved RD slightly [21].
  - Video: decoder pruning + layer-wise distillation gives 65% fewer MACs, 2x speed, under
    0.3 dB loss [22].
- **Slimmable codecs:** one network that runs at several widths (SlimCAE) [23].

**What it means for us [ours]:**
- Pruning makes a codec **faster or smaller at about the same quality**. It does not make a
  small codec compress better than the big one it came from.
- Our problem today is compression (1.7x x264), not speed. So the useful version is:
  **train a much bigger teacher, then distil or mask-decay it into our current size.** The
  student can beat a same-size model trained from scratch (EVC's claim).
- A big teacher costs a lot on a laptop: a 4x wider model is roughly 10x+ the compute, so
  days per run become weeks. That is the real constraint.
- **Cheaper relatives of the same idea:**
  - Re-parameterization (Part 2): "big at training, small at deployment", exact, no
    separate teacher run.
  - A big **encoder** only: the decoder stays small and real-time, and the encoder is not on
    the decode path at all.
  - Distil from a **public pretrained codec** (CompressAI, DCVC weights) instead of training
    our own teacher. That needs a weights download, which you would approve first.

## Part 4. Ideas (many, ranked)

Cost is laptop time. "Risk" is the chance it fails its gate. All gains are guesses from the
literature **[ours]** until measured.

### A. High value, well supported

1. **Feature propagation.** Pass a 32-48 channel feature map from frame to frame alongside
   the picture. The P-coder's context net reads both. This is the biggest known gap (Part 1).
2. **Previous-latent prior.** Feed the previous frame's decoded latent `y_{t-1}` into the
   prior net. It is free at the decoder, costs no bits, and is DCVC-HEM's core trick.
3. **Checkerboard + channel-group entropy model** for P-frames (and later I-frames).
4. **Long-chain training.** Unroll 3 → 7+ using grouped gradients (ECVC's PCFS) so it fits
   in 8 GB. It attacks drift directly and may allow GOP 60+.
5. **Hierarchical quality.** Train with a repeating λ pattern (e.g. weights 1.2, 0.5, 0.9,
   0.5). Every second frame is cheaper and leans on a better neighbour.
6. **A better I-frame model.** I-frames are small at GOP 30 but they seed every chain. Our
   older intra model was +88% vs x264 all-intra, and the current one has not been re-measured.
   Public factorized models are at -23%.

### B. Cheap wins, no or little training

7. **Encoder-side latent refinement.** At encode time, run a few gradient steps on the
   latent itself to lower rate+distortion. The decoder is unchanged, so it stays real-time
   to decode. Published for images (SGA, SGA+) and video [24].
8. **Rate-distortion-optimized rounding.** Instead of plain rounding, try the neighbouring
   integer for each latent and keep the cheaper one when distortion allows. A lighter
   version of 7.
9. **Longer / adaptive GOP.** Test GOP 60 and "infinite". Insert an I-frame only on a scene
   cut or when P-frame cost spikes.
10. **Skip mode.** A per-block flag: "copy the warped reference, send nothing". Static
    backgrounds then cost near zero.
11. **Fix the scale tables.** Use the P-coder's real symbol statistics per scale bucket
    instead of an ideal Laplace. Likely worth <1%, but it is free.
12. **Re-parameterized convs.** Fine-tune with 3-branch blocks, merge for deployment.

### C. Architecture changes

13. **Drop motion entirely** (DCVC-RT style): feed the unwarped previous features and let the
    net learn the alignment. Saves the motion bytes (4-10%) and the search time.
14. **Latent at 1/8 resolution** with fewer channels: more spatial detail, the DCVC-RT
    choice.
15. **Depthwise/ConvNeXt blocks** in encoder and synthesis.
16. **Asymmetric model**: 2-3x bigger encoder, same decoder.
17. **One variable-rate model** (λ as an input) to replace three. It trains on 3x the data
    per weight and gives smooth rate control.
18. **Two reference frames** (t-1 and t-2) for occlusions.

### D. Train-big ideas

19. **Big teacher → mask decay / distillation** to our size (Part 3).
20. **Slimmable codec**: one network, several widths, pick per device.
21. **Distil from a public pretrained codec.**

### E. Out of the box

22. **Hybrid: x264 base layer + our neural layer.** x264 codes the frame at low quality,
    and our P-coder codes the rest using the x264 picture as its reference. We inherit
    x264's motion, B-frames and rate control at once. Neural in-loop filters alone give
    5-6% on HEVC [25], and a conditional coder should do more. It is the fastest route to
    "beats x264", because the worst case is x264 itself.
23. **Neural post-filter on x264.** No bitstream change at all. Small gain, trivial to try.
24. **B-frames.** Code a middle frame given both neighbours. x264's default has B-frames
    and we have none, and that is part of why "+69% vs x264" is worse than "+24% vs
    x264 low-delay".
25. **Code at lower resolution + learned upsampler** at low bitrates.
26. **Per-video fine-tuning of the decoder's last layer**, sent as a few hundred bytes.
    Overfitted codecs (Cool-chic, C3) get HEVC-level results with tiny decoders [26].
27. **Perceptual tuning.** Train for MS-SSIM or a perceptual loss. We are already closer to
    x264 on MS-SSIM (+56%) than PSNR (+69%).
28. **YUV 4:2:0 input.** x264 codes half the chroma samples, and we code full RGB. Coding
    YUV420 natively removes work nobody sees. The test also compares against x264 in
    yuv420p.
29. **Noise/grain synthesis**: do not code film grain, re-add it at the decoder.
30. **Mixed-precision training** to roughly halve training time. It is not a codec gain, but
    every experiment gets cheaper.

## Part 5. Detailed plans

Every plan keeps our rule: the pass bar is written before the run.

### Plan 1. Feature propagation + previous-latent prior (ideas 1, 2) — **recommended next**

- **Why first:** the largest gap to the published codecs, and it needs no new training data.
- **Build:**
  - The P-coder gets a second output: a 32-channel feature map `F_t` from the layer before
    the final conv of `refine`.
  - The next frame's context net takes `[x_warp, warp(F_{t-1})]` (the same block motion
    warps the features). New input weights start at zero, so step 0 equals today's coder.
  - The prior net also takes `y_{t-1}` (decoded latent, upsampled to match). Zero init.
  - For the first P-frame after an I-frame, `F` and `y` are zeros plus a learned flag.
- **Gate 1 (local, ~1 day):** DAVIS TRAIN, unroll 3, same fine-tune for a plain copy and
  the new copy. Score on VAL-B, GOP 10 chained. **Pass: P-frame BD-rate ≤ -10%.**
- **Gate 2 (~1.5 days):** Vimeo, 10 chunks, unroll 3 → 5.
- **Gate 3:** real bytes. **Pass: beat the current best by > 8 points.**
- **Risk:** features drift over long chains (DCVC-FM's problem). Check GOP 30 explicitly.

### Plan 2. Long chains + hierarchical quality (ideas 4, 5) — training only, no new parts

- **Build:** unroll 7 with gradients cut every 3 frames (fits 8 GB); λ pattern
  [1.2, 0.5, 0.9, 0.5] repeating; re-download Vimeo (7-frame clips are exactly this).
- **Gate:** real bytes at GOP 30 and GOP 60. **Pass: > 5 points better than +69.1%.**
- **Cost:** ~1.5-2 days of training. It can be combined with Plan 1's gate 2 to save a run,
  at the cost of not knowing which part helped.

### Plan 3. Encoder-side refinement (ideas 7, 8) — no training

- **Build:** in `p1_gate3.py`'s encoder only: 5-10 Adam steps on `y` per P-frame against
  D + λR with the decoder frozen. Use the refined `y` for both the bitstream and the next
  reference.
- **Gate:** real bytes, GOP 30. **Pass: ≥ 3 points.** Report encode time (it will rise a lot;
  decode time must not change).
- **Cost:** half a day. It is independent of every other plan, so the gain should stack.

### Plan 4. Checkerboard + channel groups (idea 3)

- **Build:** split the P latent into 2 channel groups x 2 checkerboard halves = 4 coding
  passes. Each pass's (mean, scale) net sees the earlier passes. Decode stays parallel.
- **Gate 1:** equal fine-tune, VAL-B. **Pass: ≤ -6%.** Then Vimeo + real bytes.
- **Cost:** ~2 days to gate 1. The range-coder ordering needs care for exact decoding.

### Plan 5. Hybrid x264 + neural layer (idea 22) — the out-of-the-box bet

- **Build:** encode with x264 at a high CRF (cheap, blurry). Feed the decoded x264 frame as
  the P-coder's reference instead of `x_warp`. Fine-tune the P-coder on (frame, x264 frame)
  pairs. Total bytes = x264 bytes + our bytes.
- **Oracle first (half a day, no training):** run today's P-coder with the x264 frame as
  reference at 3 CRFs. **Pass to continue: total bits within 30% of x264 alone at the same
  PSNR**, before any fine-tune.
- **Why it could win:** x264's motion, B-frames and mode decisions are free to us. The
  neural layer only has to fix what x264 got wrong.
- **Risk:** it is no longer a "pure" neural codec. That is a product choice.

### Plan 6. Big teacher → small student (idea 19)

- **Only after** Plans 1-4 settle the architecture. Distilling into a shape we then change
  wastes the teacher.
- **Build:** teacher at 2x width (~35M weights), trained on Vimeo (~5-7 days on the
  laptop, or cloud). Student = our size, trained with EVC-style mask decay or feature
  distillation.
- **Gate:** student vs same-size model trained equally without a teacher.
  **Pass: ≤ -5% BD-rate.**

### Suggested order [ours]

1. The hyperprior gate finished: not passed (-0.4%). **Start with Plan A (Part 0).**
2. **Plan 3** (half a day, no training): quick and independent.
3. **Plan 5's oracle** (half a day): tells us if the hybrid is worth anything.
4. **Plan 1** (the big one), with Plan 2's training recipe in its gate 2.
5. Plan 4, then Plan 6.

## Part 6. Beat x264: the direct roadmap (added after "beat h264 at all costs")

### Where exactly we stand

| Compared with | Our codec (GOP 30) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| x264 default (medium, B-frames, long GOP) | **+69.1%** | The hard target. |
| x264 low-delay (P only, no B-frames) | **+24.0%** | **Optimistic**: that x264 run was forced to an I-frame every 10. At 30 it will do better. Must be re-run. |

- The published claim "beats H.264" usually means the second row. DVC, the first
  end-to-end learned codec, reported 19% fewer bits than H.264 in a low-delay P setup
  **[paper]** [27].
- The gap between the two rows is x264's B-frames and long GOP. For HEVC, random access is
  about 30% better than low delay **[paper]** [28].
- So there are two finish lines:
  - **Line A: beat x264 low-delay P** (the standard research claim). We need about -20% to
    -30% more, once the x264 run is redone fairly.
  - **Line B: beat x264 default.** We need about -41% (1.69x → 1.0x).

### What gets us there, in order of certainty [ours]

| Step | Expected | Certainty | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Re-run x264 low-delay at an I-frame every 30 (fair Line A number) | tells us the real gap | certain | 1-2 h, CPU only |
| 1. Encoder-side latent refinement (Plan 3) | 2-6% (one video paper measured 2.35%, and 6.2% with per-frame RD adjustment [38]) | high | 0.5 day |
| 2. Feature propagation + previous-latent prior (Plan 1) | 15-30% | medium-high | 4-5 days |
| 3. Long chains + hierarchical quality (Plan 2) | 5-12% | medium | folded into step 2's training |
| 4. Checkerboard + channel-group entropy model (Plan 4) | 6-12% | medium | 3-4 days |
| 5. B-frames (learned, or via the hybrid) | 15-28% **[paper]** [29] | medium | 1 week |
| 6. Hybrid x264 + neural layer (Plan 5) | floor = x264 itself | oracle decides | 0.5 day to know |

- Gains multiply, they do not add. Steps 1-4 at their midpoints: 0.95 x 0.78 x 0.92 x 0.91
  ≈ 0.62, i.e. about -38%. **That would cross Line A clearly and land near Line B.**
  Step 5 or 6 is what would cross Line B with margin.
- These are literature-based guesses. Each one still has to pass its own gate.

### Shortcuts, if "at all costs" includes them (your call)

- **A. The hybrid (Plan 5).** x264 inside our codec. If the neural layer saves anything at
  all over x264 at the same quality, the combined codec beats x264 by construction. It is
  the surest route to Line B. The cost is that it is no longer a pure neural codec.
- **B. Open training code for DCVC.** OpenDCVCs publishes full PyTorch training pipelines for
  DCVC, TCM, HEM and DC [30]. We could port their feature-propagation and entropy-model
  code instead of writing our own. That means downloading and reading third-party code. It
  would shorten step 2 and step 4.
- **C. Public pretrained weights (Microsoft DCVC).** They already beat x265. Using them as a
  teacher (Plan 6), or as a yardstick on our test set, is legitimate. Shipping them as "our
  codec" is not our work. It also needs a download that you approve.
- **D. Cloud GPU.** The laptop takes 1.5 days per Vimeo pass. A rented 24 GB GPU would do it
  in a few hours and would allow unroll 7+ and a wider model. That costs money.

### What I would not do

- Tune the comparison instead of the codec (weaker x264 preset, different metric). It would
  "beat x264" on paper only.
- Transformers, dense learned flow, more quantizer tuning: slow, or already tested and
  closed here.

## Part 7. Extra findings from the last searches

- **Quantization during training.** Training with added noise leaves a train/test mismatch.
  A "soft then hard" two-stage recipe reported 8.9% BD-rate savings on an image codec
  **[paper]** [31]. We already use the mixed recipe (noise for the rate, straight-through
  rounding for the decoder), so our share of that gain is smaller. A cheap extra is a final
  fine-tune of the decoder side only, with real rounding and the encoder frozen.
- **Entropy skipping (AlphaVC).** When the predicted spread of a latent is tiny, do not code
  it at all and use the predicted mean. It saved bits and cut entropy-coding time
  **[paper]** [32]. Our scale floor of 0.11 still spends bits on such latents. This is
  idea 10 (skip mode) with a published recipe, and it needs no training to try.
- **Scene cuts.** Implicit-context codecs like DCVC-RT lose quality sharply at a scene cut
  **[paper]** [33]. Idea 9 (insert an I-frame on a cost spike) matters more as the GOP
  grows.
- **Training data.** Vimeo-90K has small, smooth motion; several papers add bigger or
  higher-motion sets (YouTube-NT ~300k clips, Kinetics, 32-frame Vimeo-style clips)
  **[paper]** [34]. Our test set (DAVIS) is high-motion. Fine-tuning on DAVIS-like motion,
  or on longer clips, is a data-side lever we have not pulled. DAVIS TRAIN is already on
  disk.
- **DCVC-RT training** uses 7-frame clips: frame 1 trains the I model, and frames 2-7 train
  the P model with the previous frame's decoded features concatenated at both ends
  (as described by a paper that follows its design) **[paper]** [33]. This is the recipe
  for Plan 1.

## Part 8. What each part is worth, from the papers' own ablations

These are "bits go up by X% if you remove it" numbers from each paper's ablation, on their
codecs and test sets **[paper]**. They do not transfer one-to-one to ours, but they rank
the ideas.

| Part | Cost of removing it | Source | Our idea no. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spatial context in the entropy model (dual prior) | **+14.1%** (plain checkerboard recovers about 8 of those points) | DCVC-HEM [2] | 3 |
| Quadtree entropy coding instead of dual prior | +6.6% more | DCVC-DC [3] | 3 |
| Per-position, per-channel quantization step | **+8.9%** (whole multi-granularity scheme: +11.8%) | DCVC-HEM [2] | new: 31 |
| Previous-latent prior | about +10.1% (secondary summary, unconfirmed) | DCVC-HEM [2] | 2 |
| Temporal prior in the entropy model | +11.2% | original DCVC [35] | we have this |
| Hierarchical quality across frames | **+8.4%** | DCVC-DC [3] | 5 |
| Diverse motion offsets with cross-group mixing | +6.3% | DCVC-DC [3] | (motion: closed for us) |
| One model for I- and P-frames that adapts to reference quality | -12.1% vs DCVC-RT | UIIC [36] | 17 |
| Reward/bit allocation across frames | 4-16% depending on design | rate-control paper [37] | 5, 9 |

**New idea 31: learned quantization step per position and channel.** The entropy model
outputs a step size along with mean and scale. Flat areas get coarse steps and detailed
areas get fine ones. DCVC-HEM credits it with ~9%, and it also gives smooth rate control
from one model.

**What this changes in the ranking [ours]:**
- The **entropy model** is the richest area: spatial context (~8-14%), quantization step
  (~9%) and previous-latent prior (~10%) are all cheap at decode time, and all three live in
  the same small `prior` net. They should be built together as **one "entropy model v2"
  project** in place of the separate Plans 1b and 4.
- Hierarchical quality (~8%) is training-only and nearly free.
- Feature propagation has no clean published ablation number. A later paper (DCMVC) even
  argues the decoded picture is the cleaner reference and mixes both. So it stays a strong
  bet but not a certain one, and its gate must compare against the picture-only coder.

### Revised order to beat x264 [ours]

0. **Plan A: lift the quality ceiling** (Part 0). Measured on our own codec, so it goes
   first.
1. **Encoder-side latent refinement** (no training, 0.5 day).
2. **Entropy model v2**: checkerboard spatial context + previous-latent prior + learned
   quantization step, trained with **hierarchical quality** and **longer chains**.
   One build, one Vimeo pass (~1 week in all). Literature midpoint, if the parts multiply:
   0.90 x 0.90 x 0.91 x 0.92 ≈ 0.68, about **-32%**.
3. **Feature propagation** (Plan 1a), gated against step 2's coder.
4. **B-frames or the x264 hybrid** for the last stretch to Line B.

## Part 9. Recipes found for the build

- **Hierarchical quality weights.** DCVC-DC multiplies λ per P-frame by a repeating pattern
  of period 4: **(0.5, 1.2, 0.5, 0.9)**, as quoted by two follow-up papers **[paper]** [39].
  Another paper lists the same cycle starting one step later, so the phase needs checking
  against the DCVC-DC paper before we copy it.
- **Learned quantization step (DCVC-HEM)** **[paper]** [2]: three multiplied steps.
  1. A global step looked up from the rate setting (this is what makes one model serve all
     rates).
  2. A learned step per channel.
  3. A step per position and channel that the entropy model outputs for each frame.
  The encoder divides the latent by the step before rounding and the decoder multiplies it
  back. (The divide/multiply detail is the standard convention, not confirmed from their
  code.)
- **Checkerboard** **[paper]** [40]: half the positions ("anchors") are coded from the prior
  alone. The other half also see the decoded anchors through one masked conv. Two passes,
  about 2.5x faster to decode than serial context. CompressAI ships a reference
  implementation.
- **Encoder-side refinement, measured on video** **[paper]** [38]: 2.35% from refining the
  latent, 6.2% in total with a per-frame rate-distortion adjustment (HEVC classes C and D).
  That is smaller than the image results suggested, so Plan 3's bar of 3 points is about
  right and it may miss.
- **Distribution shape** **[paper]** [41]: a "generalized Gaussian" adds one shape number per
  latent and beat both Gaussian and Gaussian-mixture models in image codecs. Our Laplace is
  one fixed shape. A learned shape is a small change to the prior's output and the scale
  tables.
- **Fair-comparison check for our own benchmark** **[paper]** [42]: papers differ on RGB vs
  YUV420 scoring and on BT.601 vs BT.709 conversion, and the choice moves results by many
  points. We should confirm which conversion our x264 path uses and state it next to every
  "vs x264" number. This could move our number in either direction.

## Part 10. Two findings that change the plan

### B-frames by reusing our P-frame coder (B-EPIC)

- **[paper]** [29]: to code a frame between two already-decoded frames, first *interpolate*
  those two into one synthetic reference, then run the **existing P-frame coder** with that
  reference in place of the previous frame. The same network does both jobs. It reports
  **28.5% fewer bits than the P-frame codec** on UVG at the same quality.
- **Why it fits us [ours]:** our P-coder takes "a reference picture" as input and does not
  care where it came from. A B-frame mode needs only (a) a reference made from two frames
  and (b) a new frame order. A no-training oracle exists: build the reference as the
  average of two block-motion warps (from the past frame and from the future frame) and
  code with today's coder.
- This is also the feature x264's default uses and we lack. It moves from "last stretch"
  to **a top candidate**.
- **Cost:** it adds delay (the future frame must be coded first), so it is not "low-delay".
  For streaming of stored video that is fine. For live calls it is not.

**Plan 7. B-frame oracle (half a day, no training).**
- Frame order per group of 4: code frame 4 as a P-frame from frame 0, then frame 2 from
  (0, 4), then frames 1 and 3 from their neighbours.
- Reference = average of the two block-warped neighbours. Motion bytes are paid for both
  vectors.
- **Pass to continue:** total bits at least 8% lower than the all-P chain at the same PSNR,
  with today's stage 3 coder and no fine-tuning. A fine-tune and Vimeo training follow if
  it passes.

### Our "vs x264" number depends on the colour handling

- Our benchmark gives x264 the frames as 4:2:0 (half the colour samples, FFmpeg's default
  conversion) and scores everything in RGB (`scripts/benchmark_parity.py`). Our codec codes
  full RGB.
- At high quality x264 therefore hits a ceiling from the colour subsampling that we do not
  have, which helps us in RGB scoring. Scored the way codec papers score YUV 4:2:0
  **[paper]** [42], our gap to x264 could be larger than +69%.
- This does not change any decision today, because every one of our comparisons uses the same
  setup. But a claim of "beats x264" should be checked in both scorings before we say it.
  Adding a 4:2:0-scored column to the benchmark is about half a day.

## Part 11. Smaller levers and one caution

- **Caution on feature propagation.** The original DCVC ablation reports that conditioning
  on context *features* instead of an RGB prediction was worth **4.6%** **[paper]** [35]
  (the snippet was cut off, so the baseline is unconfirmed). That is far below what the
  later papers' totals suggest. It supports putting **entropy model v2 and B-frames ahead
  of feature propagation**, as the revised order does.
- **Learning-rate schedule.** A better decay schedule was worth 1-2% in an image codec, even
  with fewer steps **[paper]** [43]. We halve the rate every 2 chunks. A final low-rate
  stage (1e-5) is a common recipe and costs a few hours.
- **Crop size.** Evidence is mixed: one controlled test favoured small crops with bigger
  batches, another found 512 crops better late in training **[paper]** [44]. Not worth a
  run for us now.
- **EMA of weights.** I found no clean evidence that it helps compression. Skip it.
- **Training speed.** Mixed precision with channels-last memory format is the standard
  route to faster conv training. Reported gains vary, and it can even be slower on some
  models **[paper]** [45]. Our GDN layers divide by a learned norm, so half precision needs
  a stability check first. It is worth a 1-hour benchmark because every plan above is
  limited by laptop training time.
- **Conditional vs residual coding.** Theory and ablations agree that conditional coding
  (ours) is at least as good as residual coding **[paper]** [46]. Our P-coder already has
  this right, so nothing needs to change here.

## Part 12. Late findings: per-video tuning, mode choice, two-frame coding

- **Per-video tuning ("instance-adaptive")** **[paper]** [47]: fine-tune the whole codec on
  the video being coded, and send the small weight changes inside the file, entropy-coded.
  Reported **21-27% BD-rate savings** on a learned P-frame codec and 17-20% on a B-frame
  codec, on UVG/HEVC/Xiph. It still worked with the network shrunk by 70%.
  - **Fit for us [ours]:** the decoder runs at the same speed, since it just loads updated
    weights first. Encoding becomes minutes per video instead of seconds. That fits
    "encode once, play many times" (stored video) and does not fit live video.
  - Together with the B-frame route (Part 10), it is one of the two largest published
    gains in this survey that need **no new architecture and no Vimeo training**. The cost is encode time and the bytes for the
    weight changes, which matter more on short clips (our test clips are ~80 frames, where
    their datasets have hundreds of frames, so our gain could be smaller).
  - **Plan 8. Per-video tuning oracle (1 day).** For each test video: fine-tune only the
    synthesis+refine biases and the prior's last layer for N steps on that video's own
    frames, quantize the changes to 8 bits, and count their bytes with a simple entropy
    estimate. **Pass: ≥ 8 points better vs x264 with the weight bytes included.** Encode
    time is reported.
- **Mode choice per block (MMVC)** **[paper]** [48]: several predictors, and each block
  picks the one with the cheapest residual. That is the learned version of x264's mode
  decision, and of our skip-mode idea (10). The one-bit map costs less than it saves.
- **Coding two frames together (UI²C)** **[paper]** [36]: one model codes frames in pairs,
  using both directions, with no separate I-frame model, and learns to fall back to
  "intra" when the reference is bad (trained with blank or noisy references). 12.1% better
  than DCVC-RT. For us this argues for training the P-coder with **occasionally corrupted
  or blank references**, so one coder handles scene cuts and I-frames. That is a cheap
  training change.
- **Hierarchical B-frames in H.264** **[paper]** [49]: about 10% fewer bits than plain
  IBBP with groups of 8 or more. DCVC-B (learned B-frames with hierarchical quality) is
  26.6% better than HEVC's reference software in random access **[paper]** [50].

### Final ranking for "beat x264" [ours]

| Rank | Step | Why here | Time to a yes/no |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Plan A: wider latent** (Part 0) | The only item backed by a measurement on our codec: two videos are stuck at ~28.5 dB even with free bits | 1 day |
| 1 | **B-frame oracle** (Plan 7) | No training; x264 has it and we lack it; 28.5% in the paper | 0.5 day |
| 2 | **Per-video tuning oracle** (Plan 8) | No new architecture; 21-27% in the paper | 1 day |
| 3 | **Encoder-side latent refinement** (Plan 3) | No training; small but stacks | 0.5 day |
| 4 | **Entropy model v2 + hierarchical quality + longer chains** | Best-supported trained change, ~-30% | ~1 week |
| 5 | Feature propagation | Weaker evidence than I first thought | ~1 week |
| 6 | x264 hybrid | Oracle run on 3 videos: fails on the detailed ones until the capacity fix is in | done (Part 0) |

Three of the top four need **no Vimeo training**, so they can each be answered in a day or
less. If the paper numbers held even at half strength and multiplied
(0.86 x 0.88 x 0.97 x 0.84 ≈ 0.62), that is about -38%: enough for Line A and close to
Line B.

## Part 13. Last findings: how much size buys, and a free variant of per-video tuning

### Size buys very little by itself (matters for "train huge, then prune")

- An image-codec scaling study (68.5M → 1B weights) fits loss ∝ N^-0.0147 **[paper]** [51].
  With that exponent, **10x more weights lowers the loss by only about 3%**. Their forecast
  is ~30% fewer bits than VTM at 10 **billion** weights.
- NVC-1B scaled a video codec from ~21M to 1B weights and reports about -25% vs VTM
  **[paper]** [52]. That is ~50x the size for a gain that architecture changes
  (DCVC-DC → FM → RT) delivered at constant or smaller size.
- Distillation keeps a much smaller student within ~1.2% of its teacher (FEDS: ~63-67% fewer
  weights) **[paper]** [53]. So shrinking works, but the teacher has to be good first.
- **Conclusion [ours]:** for our goal, "bigger, then prune" is a poor trade on a laptop.
  A 10x model would take weeks to train for a few percent. The same weeks spent on the
  entropy model, B-frames and per-video tuning are each worth 10-30% in the papers. Use
  pruning/distillation later, to make the final codec fast, not to make it compress better.

### Per-video tuning with zero extra bytes: tune the encoder only

- **[paper]** [54]: fine-tune only the **encoder** (or small low-rank adapters on it) on the
  video being coded. The decoder is untouched, so **nothing extra is sent**. Lu et al.
  introduced this for video. A later paper changes under 10% of the encoder's weights with
  adapters. A LoRA-style version reports BD-rate gains on UVG, MCL-JCV and HEVC.
- **Fit for us [ours]:** this removes Plan 8's main risk on short clips (the bytes for the
  weight changes). It is the heavier cousin of latent refinement (Plan 3): refinement
  tunes each frame's latent, and this tunes the encoder for the whole clip.
- **Plan 8 revised:** run two arms.
  - (a) Encoder-only tuning, with no extra bytes.
  - (b) Decoder-bias tuning, with the weight bytes counted.
  The pass bar stays at ≥ 8 points vs x264. Arm (a) alone is half a day.

## Part 14. Cheap classical upgrades to our block motion

Our motion is 16x16 blocks at whole-pixel steps with hard block edges. x264 uses
quarter-pixel steps, and older codecs gained from blending block edges.

- **Quarter-pixel motion** **[paper]** [55]: reported as about 1.5 dB better than half-pixel
  at medium bitrates in H.264 reference tests (a mailing-list recollection of published
  results, not a table I could read). No clean integer-vs-quarter number turned up.
- **Overlapped block motion (OBMC)** **[paper]** [56]: blending neighbouring blocks'
  predictions. Gains range from under 1% in modern codecs to 5-18% against plain block
  motion. It also removes block-edge jumps in the prediction.
- **What our own tests say [ours]:** our motion test found that a smoother, more exact
  reference is worth up to 19.9% once the coder is trained on it, when the motion is free.
  Quarter-pixel 16x16 vectors with blended edges are a *cheap* way to get part of that:
  about 4 extra bits per block (~1,200 bits per frame, on top of ~1,800 today) against
  ~25,000 bits of residual.
- **Idea 32 / Plan 9. Sub-pixel + overlapped block motion oracle (half a day).**
  Add arms to `p2_motion_oracle.py`: `block16_qpel` and `block16_qpel_obmc`, with motion
  bits paid. Fine-tune a coder copy on each, as the fairness check did.
  **Pass: ≤ -8% P-frame BD-rate vs block16 with an equally tuned coder.**
  This is inside the motion line we closed, so it is worth reopening only because the cost
  is so much lower than learned flow.

## Part 15. Proof that small, fast neural codecs beat H.264

- **ELF-VC** (WaveOne, ICCV 2021) **[paper]** [57]: low-latency (I and P frames only), one
  model for all rates, fewer weights than other learned codecs of its time. On UVG under
  PSNR: **44% fewer bits than H.264**, 26% fewer than H.265. Speed on a 2017 GPU: 720p at
  19 fps encode / 35 fps decode. Its named ingredients: a flexible-rate single model, an
  efficient backbone, and **predicting the motion from past frames inside the loop** so
  less motion has to be sent.
- **MobileNVC** (Qualcomm, 2023) **[paper]** [58]: real-time 1080p decoding on a phone, and
  it uses **block-based motion compensation**, like ours, because phones have fast hardware
  for it. It is 48% better than its predecessor at a tenth of the compute.
- **DCVC-UF** (Microsoft, CVPR 2026) **[paper]** [59]: codes a **chunk of several frames into
  one latent** and decodes them in parallel. Reported 371/274 fps at 1080p on a 4090 and
  42.2% fewer bits than VTM low-delay (secondary summaries; not checked against the paper's
  tables).
- **Cool-chic video** **[paper]** [60]: 800 weights in total, about 900 multiplications per
  pixel, quality close to H.264. It is overfitted per video, which is the extreme form of
  per-video tuning.

**What this tells us [ours]:**
- Beating H.264 by a wide margin with a small low-delay model is a solved problem in the
  literature (ELF-VC did it in 2021 without B-frames). Our gap is execution, not a wall.
- Block motion is not what holds us back (MobileNVC keeps it).
- New idea 33: **chunk coding**. Code 2-4 frames as one latent with 3D or stacked-channel
  convs. It shares one set of side information across frames and it is the newest
  direction from the DCVC group. It is a full rebuild, so it comes after the items above.
- New idea 34: **predict the motion from the past** (ELF-VC). Send only the correction to a
  motion field predicted from the two previous frames. Our motion bytes are 4-10% of the
  stream, so the ceiling is small.

## Part 16. Build recipes for the top-ranked steps

- **Dual spatial prior, exact recipe (DCVC-HEM)** **[paper]** [2]:
  1. Split the latent's channels into two halves.
  2. Step 1: code the **even** checkerboard positions of half A and the **odd** positions
     of half B, from the prior alone. Uncoded positions are zeros.
  3. Fuse what was decoded and feed it to a small net that outputs new (mean, scale).
  4. Step 2: code the remaining positions of both halves.
  - Every position sees neighbours from step 1, and each half sees the other half at the
    same position. Two passes, no added delay over plain checkerboard. This is the +14.1%
    row in Part 8, and it is the concrete design for "entropy model v2".
- **B-frames from a P-coder (B-EPIC)** **[paper]** [29]: the interpolator is Super-SloMo
  (a small flow net plus a refine net; it can interpolate at any time point, not only the
  middle). Both nets train jointly with the codec. The paper compares frame orders
  (sequential vs hierarchical) and group shapes (I-B-I vs I-B-P). Our oracle can start with
  plain averaged block warps and add a small learned blend net only if the oracle passes.
- **Encoder tuning per video (Lu et al.)** **[paper]** [61]: freeze the decoder and take a
  few gradient steps on the encoder per frame. Their single-frame example: 0.056 → 0.051 bpp
  (-9%) with +0.07 dB. Their full method (with error-propagation-aware training) is
  -3.8% to -15.9% BD-rate across HEVC classes. The isolated gain of the encoder update was
  not in the snippets.
- **Adapters for per-video tuning (LoRA-style)** **[paper]** [62]: faster than full
  fine-tuning. The authors note it gets *worse at low bitrates*, because the adapter bytes
  weigh more there. That is the same risk I flagged for our short clips, and it is another
  reason to test the encoder-only arm first.
- **MobileNVC's motion** **[paper]** [58]: block warping plus a **flow extrapolator** that
  predicts the next block motion from past ones, with 8-bit integer weights. It is a
  working example of idea 34 on top of block motion like ours.
