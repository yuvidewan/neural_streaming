## What was tested

- **Project stage:** Milestone 6.5+ — full still-image codec (`BaselineAutoencoder` + quantization + entropy coding + `.nvc` binary format), plus the Vimeo-90K large-scale training pipeline.

All 10 Vimeo chunks completed. Best checkpoint landed at **epoch 17**, with a best validation MSE of **0.000915** on Vimeo's own held-out chunk data during training (not directly comparable to the DAVIS numbers below — different dataset, different scale).

### Headline result: cross-dataset generalization test

Measured on all 719 real DAVIS test frames, real `.nvc` files:

| Bits | PSNR (dB) | Total BPP | Compression ratio | Clipped |

**Losslessness of entropy coding verified `true` at every bit depth** — decoded symbols exactly match encoded ones, and the `.nvc` path matches the direct quantize/dequantize path bit-for-bit, so nothing here is silently corrupted.

### How this compares to the original DAVIS-trained baseline

The README documents the *original* baseline (trained **on** DAVIS) benchmarked on the same 719-frame DAVIS test split:

| Bits | New (Vimeo-trained) | Original (DAVIS-trained) | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 26.654 dB / 1.9146 BPP | 27.167 dB / 1.8844 BPP | **−0.51 dB** |

This is a genuinely good result, honestly read: a model that has **never seen DAVIS** comes within about half a dB of a model trained directly on it, at essentially the same bitrate. That's the expected shape of a domain-generalization gap — better in-distribution, moderately worse out-of-distribution — and the gap is small, not a collapse. It's real evidence the encoder learned something about natural video structure that transfers, not something DAVIS-specific.

### Visual check

Eight consecutive frames from DAVIS's `bmx-bumps` sequence were inspected directly. Scene structure, color, and the rider/bike shapes are all preserved correctly — no corruption, no color drift, no blocking artifacts. The reconstructions are visibly **softer/blurrier** than the originals, most noticeably on fine texture (the chain-link fence, grass texture) — consistent with a plain-MSE-trained autoencoder with no perceptual or adversarial loss, and consistent with the ~26-27 dB PSNR range. This matches what the numbers predict; nothing looks visually broken.

## In plain terms

Think of this project as a smart image-squeezing tool: it looks at a video frame, boils it down to a compact "sketch" of the important information, squeezes that sketch into a small file, and can then rebuild a picture from that small file later. The question this test answers is: **if you only ever showed this tool one library of videos (Vimeo) during practice, how well does it handle a completely different library of videos (DAVIS) it's never seen before?**

The short answer: **surprisingly well.** It shrunk the DAVIS video frames down to somewhere between 1/12th and 1/27th of their original size (depending on a quality dial we can turn), and when we rebuilt the pictures from those tiny files, they still looked like the original scene — right colors, right shapes, right layout — just slightly softer, like a photo that's very slightly out of focus rather than sharp. And a model that had specifically practiced on DAVIS beforehand only did marginally better (about half a decibel, a small technical unit of picture quality) — so this "never seen it before" model isn't far behind at all.

We also double-checked that the squeezing-and-unsqueezing step itself doesn't lose or corrupt any extra information beyond the expected quality dial — it's mathematically confirmed to reconstruct exactly what it's supposed to, no silent bugs.

### Pros
- **Generalizes well.** It wasn't just memorizing the videos it trained on — it learned something more general about what video frames look like, and that transferred to a brand-new dataset.
- **Real, meaningful compression.** Files came out 12x to almost 27x smaller than the raw, uncompressed picture, with a dial to trade off "smaller file" against "sharper picture."
- **No hidden corruption.** The compression step was proven to be exact — what gets decompressed is exactly what should come out, not an approximation with silent bugs.
- **The compression math is near-perfect.** The part of the system responsible for actually packing the data efficiently is working about as well as it theoretically can.
- **Flexible.** You can dial quality up or down (8-bit, 6-bit, 4-bit) depending on whether you care more about picture quality or file size.

### Cons / limitations
- **Some quality is lost.** Pictures come back slightly blurry compared to the original — fine, but not perfect. Zoomed-in details (like a fence pattern) lose crispness.
- **Not fast enough for real video yet.** Each frame takes on the order of a tenth of a second to compress or decompress on a regular computer — fine for testing, but far too slow for something like real-time video calls or streaming.
- **Still image-by-image, not true video compression.** It compresses each frame on its own; it doesn't yet take advantage of the fact that consecutive video frames look almost identical to each other, which is how real video compression (like what YouTube or Netflix uses) gets much better results.
- **Slightly behind a specialist.** A version of this tool trained specifically on DAVIS still edges it out a little — expected, but worth knowing this isn't quite as good as a dataset-specific version.
- **A small sliver of data gets rounded away.** Under 1% of values had to be trimmed to fit the compression grid — a deliberate, measured trade-off, not a bug, but it's not perfectly lossless end-to-end (only the *compression step itself* is lossless; the earlier "shrink it down" step is where the acceptable, expected quality loss happens).
